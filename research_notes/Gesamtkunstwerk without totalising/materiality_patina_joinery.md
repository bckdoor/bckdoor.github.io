# Materiality, Patina and Joinery as Design Language ("precision holds patina")

Scope note: this was researched in about 22 web searches. Most results came back as summaries, not full pages. Wherever a book page reference or exact quote could not be checked against a primary text, it is flagged. Many material-ageing timelines come from suppliers or retailers. They are labelled as industry or marketing claims, not conservation science.

## 1. Philosophy of patina and ageing: what do the canonical texts actually say?

### Takeaway
Writing on patina repeatedly treats visible age as accumulated use and handling ("the glow of grime", "voicefulness", Alterswert). Writing on the joint treats the detail as where construction becomes meaning (Frascari: construction + construing; Frampton: the poetics of construction). Together they make a coherent intellectual basis for "precision holds patina": the precise joint is the readable, constant element, and the surface is the changing, time-recording element. Several popular quotes are unreliable or vary by translation (see Gaps). The Scarpa "joint is the beginning of ornament" line could not be verified.

### Cited Findings
**Tanizaki, *In Praise of Shadows* (1933)**
- The best-known passage, in one translation: "We do not dislike everything that shines, but we do prefer a pensive luster to a shallow brilliance". It goes on: the much-praised "sheen of antiquity" "is in fact the glow of grime". Another translation reads "lustre ... a murky light that, whether in stone or an artefact, bespeaks a sheen of antiquity." — [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/229872052); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_Praise_of_Shadows)
- Tanizaki ties the glow to touch. The words for it describe a polish that "comes of being touched over and over again", meaning a sheen built up from skin oils over years of handling. — [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/229872052)
- In another translation: "For better or for worse we do love things that bear the marks of grime, soot, and weather, and we love the colors and the sheen that call to mind the past that made them." Wording changes noticeably between translations (Harper/Seidensticker 1977 is the standard English edition), so cite one translation consistently. — [Goodreads quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/229872052)
- Caution: some reworked web texts attribute claims to Tanizaki that are not in the essay. One example is fightcancer.org's material on polishing silver. Do not cite such pages. — [search assessment; page not cited]

**Wabi-sabi: Leonard Koren, *Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers* (1994)**
- Koren's definition: "a beauty of things imperfect, impermanent, and incomplete. It is a beauty of things modest and humble. It is a beauty of things unconventional." — [Spirituality & Practice review](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/books/reviews/view/4545); [TWiki WabiSabi excerpt](https://wiki.gnhlug.org/TWiki/WabiSabi)
- Koren's three "lessons gleaned from nature": "All things are impermanent. All things are imperfect. All things are incomplete." — [Stella Kalaw Substack](https://stellakalaw.substack.com/p/imperfect-impermanent-and-incomplete); [Spirituality & Practice](https://www.spiritualityandpractice.com/books/books.php?id=4545)
- The material qualities often listed (suggestion of natural process, irregularity, intimacy, unpretentiousness, earthiness, simplicity) appear on wiki pages that may paraphrase Koren. Check them against the book before quoting. — [TUM foswiki WabiSabi](https://foswiki.in.tum.de/System/WabiSabi)

**Ruskin, *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), ch. VI "The Lamp of Memory"**
- "For, indeed, the greatest glory of a building is not in its stones, nor in its gold. Its glory is in its Age, and in that deep sense of voicefulness, of stern watching, of mysterious sympathy ... which we feel in walls that have long been washed by the passing waves of humanity." Wording was confirmed in two reprints (snippet level). — [Victorian Web](https://victorianweb.org/authors/ruskin/luchette.html); [Monumenta reprint](https://www.monumenta.org/en/magazine/site:0XTA-XEHREV1K7/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%A7%CE%95%CE%A3%20%CE%A3%CE%95%20%CE%9B%CE%95%CE%9E%CE%95%CE%99%CE%A3/site:WFUL-ACVRFOXUD)
- Same chapter: "when we build, let us think that we build for ever." — [Victorian Web](https://victorianweb.org/authors/ruskin/luchette.html)
- Ruskin drew the idea that architecture's glory lies in its age partly from Archibald Alison. — [Wikipedia: Seven Lamps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seven_Lamps_of_Architecture)

**Alois Riegl, *Der moderne Denkmalkultus* (1903)**
- *Alterswert* (age value): a monument is valued because it visibly carries the traces of age (*Altersspuren*). Digital reconstruction is said to challenge the concept. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/BF03545661)
- Scholarship on Riegl's historiography and on the essay's slow reception outside German-speaking countries. — [Anais do Museu Paulista](https://revistas.usp.br/anaismp/article/view/186908); [Grey Room 80, "Mood for Modernists"](https://greyroom.org/issues/80/153/mood-for-modernists-an-introduction-to-three-riegl-translations-)

**Frascari, "The Tell-the-Tale Detail"**
- Core thesis: the detail joins "construction" (technical know-how guided by reason) and "construing" (reasoned discourse). Details are "minimal units of signification" in architecture. The essay opens by tracing "God lies in the detail" (Mies; with versions linked to Warburg and Flaubert). — [Cornell-hosted PDF](https://arl.human.cornell.edu/linked%20docs/Marco%20Frascari%20Tell-The-Tale%20Detail.pdf)
- Publication date conflicts. The Cornell PDF cites it in *Semiotics 1981* (Deely & Lenhart eds., Springer, pub. 1983). It is widely cited as *VIA* 7 (1984), but the VIA listing was not verified here. Virginia Tech calls it his best-known essay, reprinted and translated widely. — [Cornell PDF](https://arl.human.cornell.edu/linked%20docs/Marco%20Frascari%20Tell-The-Tale%20Detail.pdf); [VT memorial](https://news.vt.edu/articles/2013/06/060713-caus-frascari.html); [Wikipedia: Marco Frascari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Frascari)
- Frascari studied under Carlo Scarpa. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Frascari)

**Frampton, *Studies in Tectonic Culture* (MIT Press, 1995)**
- Scholarship on Scarpa's joints draws on Frampton's argument that modern architecture is to be recognised as a "poetics of construction". It reads Scarpa's architecture as a "dis-joint" of tectonic forms. — [KoreaScience paper](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201929064693773.page)
- The "joint is the beginning of ornament" attribution: NO source found confirming who said it or where. — [search result set](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201929064693773.page)

**Zumthor, *Atmospheres* (2006)**
- Secondary sources confirm themes including "body of architecture", "material compatibility", "sound of a space", "temperature of a space" and "tension between interior and exterior". The full list and page numbers were not verified. — [UAL student reflective blog](https://ariellixinrui.myblog.arts.ac.uk/?p=44); [Hacettepe thesis on Zumthor materials](https://openaccess.hacettepe.edu.tr/items/a7b4ea78-cba0-41b9-883d-8aba1629011a)

### Inferences
- Tanizaki, Ruskin and Riegl share a mechanism: value comes from time *made legible* on a surface by use. That gives the designer a client-facing message: patina is a record, not deterioration.
- Frascari's construction/construing gives the counterpart. The joint is where precision is *read*. So "precision holds patina" can be stated as: the joint construes, the surface remembers.
- Riegl's age value conflicts with "newness value" (*Neuheitswert*), which is in his essay as the opposing value. That gives a useful vocabulary for client personas who want "always new" versus "gets better". (The *Neuheitswert* term is from background knowledge and was not re-verified in this session.)

### Gaps
- **Scarpa, "the joint is the beginning of ornament":** unverified. It is commonly linked to Scarpa through Frampton's chapter on Scarpa in *Studies in Tectonic Culture*. Treat it as a paraphrase until checked against the book. Do not put it in quotation marks on client materials.
- **Pallasmaa, *The Eyes of the Skin* (1996/2005):** no passage on patina or time was retrieved. A commonly paraphrased idea, that materials such as stone, brick and wood let the eye penetrate their surfaces and show their age, needs checking against the book. Not cited.
- **Zumthor, *Thinking Architecture*:** no verified quotes retrieved, including the much-quoted passages on material "presence" and on traces of use. Check against the Birkhäuser edition.
- **Riegl:** the standard English translation is Forster & Ghirardo, *Oppositions* 25 (1982). There is also an English version in Getty's *Historical and Philosophical Issues in the Conservation of Cultural Heritage* (1996). Both are from background knowledge and were not verified in this session.
- No page references could be verified for any book.

## 2. Material behaviours: how does each material age, how long does it take, and how is it cared for?

### Takeaway
Few material timelines come from conservation science. Most come from suppliers. Defensible ranges: unlacquered brass darkens visibly in months and settles over about 1 to 5 years. Copper roofing turns brown within a few years and green over about 20 to 30 years. Yakisugi lasts decades (supplier claims of 80 to 120+ years, depending on installation). Hagi ware changes colour with use through crazing. Kaikado caddies change in months (copper) to years (tin). Calcite stones (marble, travertine, lime plaster) all react to acid. Honing hides etching but does not prevent it.

### Cited Findings
**Brass (unlacquered, "living finish")**
- Mechanism (retailer-level explanation): bare copper alloy reacts with oxygen and moisture to form cuprous oxide (Cu2O), then cupric oxide (CuO). Skin oils and trace acids in the air speed up and localise the darkening, so handled zones age differently. — [Art & Forge guide](https://artandforge.com/blogs/news/the-definitive-guide-to-unlacquered-brass-patina); [Casson living finishes guide](https://cassonhardware.com/blogs/resources/living-finishes-guide)
- Timeline conflicts. One retailer says it deepens visibly over year 1 and stabilises at about 3 to 5 years ([Casson](https://cassonhardware.com/blogs/resources/living-finishes-guide)). Another says 6 to 12 months to "full patina" in ideal conditions ([Art & Forge](https://artandforge.com/blogs/news/the-definitive-guide-to-unlacquered-brass-patina)). Humidity, temperature and handling all affect the rate.
- Care: minimal. Wipe with a soft cloth only, and avoid abrasives. Polishing with brass cleaner resets the surface to bright. Lacquer stops the change, which turns a living finish into a fixed one. — [Armac Martin finishes](https://www.armacmartin.com/collections/finishes); [Casson](https://cassonhardware.com/blogs/resources/living-finishes-guide)

**Copper (exterior)**
- The Statue of Liberty patina is mostly brochantite (basic copper sulfate). Antlerite, which is less stable and linked to acid deposition, appears in exposed zones. The 1905 sample was mainly brochantite, while the 1980 sample also contained copper chlorides and antlerite. — [Powder Diffraction (Cambridge)](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/powder-diffraction/article/an-xray-diffraction-analysis-of-the-patina-of-the-statue-of-liberty/9982128651B7D8B545D7869FA1C745D9)
- Timeline: copper colour, then brown within a few years, then green over about 20 to 30 years. A July 1906 wire report records officials worrying about the "verdigris" and discussing repainting. — [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/statue-liberty-different-color/); [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/video/why-is-the-statue-of-liberty-green-k31x0j); [History.com](https://www.history.com/news/statue-of-liberty-green-color-change)

**Kaikado tea caddies (copper, brass, tin), Kyoto, since 1875**
- Made by hand in Kyoto since 1875, in 130 to 140 steps, with a process unchanged since the company was founded. — [Yoshien](https://www.yoshien.com/en/tea-caddy-kaikado-brass-100g.html); [Wikipedia: Kaikado](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaikado)
- Precision: the lid is so close-fitting that, once aligned, it sinks closed under its own weight and pushes out the air. The airtight fit is the defining feature. — [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaikado); [Kettl](https://kettl.co/products/kaikado-120g-brass-tea-caddy-with-tea-scoop)
- Patina timeline (retailer estimate): copper begins visible change after 2 to 3 months, brass after 1 to 2 years, tin after 3 to 5 years. — [Yoshien copper/tin/brass set](https://www.yoshien.com/en/copper-tin-and-brass-tea-caddy-set.html)

**Yakisugi / shou sugi ban (charred cedar)**
- Nakamoto Forestry (supplier) gives 80 to 120 years on average. Oil finish, maintenance and UV exposure are the variables, and installation is the biggest factor: trapped moisture causes rot fast. It says a blanket "80 years without maintenance" claim is hard to pin down. — [Nakamoto Forestry longevity](https://nakamotoforestry.com/knowledge/shou-sugi-ban-longevity-how-long-does-yakisugi-last/)
- Care: re-oil periodically to keep it fresh, or let it weather to a silvered patina. In Japan, siding is often left to weather and then replaced. — [Nakamoto Forestry](https://nakamotoforestry.com/knowledge/shou-sugi-ban-longevity-how-long-does-yakisugi-last/)
- A third-party project article estimates 80 to 150 years without maintenance, with a rainscreen. — [Builder Online](https://www.builderonline.com/design/this-yakisugi-clad-passive-house-is-the-apex-of-sustainable-home-building)

**Soap-finished oak (Danish practice)**
- Soap suits pale species (white oak, ash, maple, beech) and mutes dark ones (walnut, cherry). Use it on solid wood only, because water can lift veneer. — [Popular Woodworking](https://www.popularwoodworking.com/finishing/soap-wood-finish/); [Fine Woodworking](https://finewoodworking.com/2015/02/04/lather-up-soap-flake-finish)
- Frequency varies. It may need monthly reapplication on high-use tabletops ([Popular Woodworking](https://www.popularwoodworking.com/finishing/soap-wood-finish/)); the traditional routine was an annual scrub ([Canadian Woodworking](https://canadianwoodworking.com/techniques_and_tips/soap-flakes/)). Repeated soaping builds up, lightens the wood and makes it more dirt-resistant as soap settles into the cells ([PP Møbler workshop](https://pp.dk/workshop/glue-and-surface-treatment/)).
- Sources conflict on darkening. Lye and soap whiten and resist yellowing ([From Scratch Farmstead](https://fromscratchfarmstead.com/wood-soap-finish-floors/)), but soap offers no UV protection, so oak still darkens naturally ([Fine Woodworking](https://finewoodworking.com/2015/02/04/lather-up-soap-flake-finish)). Soap is not durable against abuse, but it is easy to renew.

**Leather: vegetable-tanned vs chrome-tanned**
- Veg-tan darkens and deepens with light, oils and handling. Chrome-tan is usually evenly dyed and given heavier finish coats, so it stays largely static: scuffs, creases and edge wear, but little tonal shift. — [Von Baer](https://vonbaer.com/blogs/blog/leather-patina); [Montana Leather](https://www.montanaleather.com/chrome-vs-veg-tanned-leather-what-are-the-differences/); [Carryology](https://www.carryology.com/insights/chrome-vs-vegetable-tanned-leather/)
- Process time: veg-tanning takes weeks (sources say up to 6 weeks, or 4 to 12 weeks). Chrome tanning is faster and gives better water and heat resistance. — [Steel Horse Leather](https://steelhorseleather.com/blogs/the-journal/vegetable-tanning-vs-chrome-tanning); [Pampeano](https://www.pampeano.com/blogs/journal/vegetable-tanned-leather-vs-chrome-tanned-leather-which-one-is-better)
- Montana Leather notes that "technically, neither is better". Most patina claims come from veg-tan sellers. — [Montana Leather](https://www.montanaleather.com/chrome-vs-veg-tanned-leather-what-are-the-differences/)

**Marble and travertine, honed vs polished**
- Acid etching is chemical damage to calcite, not a stain. It shows clearly on polished stone and blends into honed stone. Honing does NOT prevent etching. — [Stone Doctor](https://stonedoctor.com.au/blogs/stone-doctor-australia/what-is-acid-etching-on-marble); [Slabwise](https://slabwise.com/guides/what-is-honing-used-for-on-marble-countertops)
- Honed finishes stop at about 200 to 800 grit and are more porous. Etches are repaired mechanically by re-honing or re-polishing. — [AZ Big Media](https://azbigmedia.com/lifestyle/home-and-design/polished-vs-honed-marble-finish-guide/); [International Stoneworks](https://intlstoneworks.com/blog/?p=395)
- Travertine is most often specified honed, and its pores are usually filled in production. Re-seal about every 3 to 5 years (industry guidance). Avoid acids such as wine, vinegar, citrus and tomato, and also bleach and ammonia. — [Bob Vila](https://www.bobvila.com/articles/travertine.md); [Stoneworld](https://stoneworld.com/articles/83331-travertine-treatment-and-maintenance)

**Lime plaster and tadelakt**
- Lime hardens by carbonation: calcium hydroxide takes up CO2 and turns back into limestone. Tadelakt is polished with stones and black (olive) soap, which forms insoluble calcium soaps, speeds surface carbonation and makes the surface water-resistant. — [Rancho Mastatal](https://ranchomastatal.com/blognewsletter/tadelakt); [Wikipedia: Tadelakt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadelakt)
- Maintenance conflicts. Re-soap regularly ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadelakt)). Wax at least yearly in wet areas ([Earthaus](https://intercom.help/earthaus-plaster/en/articles/15559956-how-do-i-care-for-and-maintain-my-plaster-walls)). Wax traps moisture, so feed with soap instead (advocacy article; [Marrakeche](https://www.marrakeche.com/?p=47481)). Kreidezeit (a manufacturer) does not recommend tadelakt for showers or basins ([Kreidezeit PDF](https://www.kreidezeit.de/wp-content/uploads/tadelakt-white.pdf)). Bleach and acidic descalers destroy or etch the surface ([Remodelista](https://www.remodelista.com/posts/remodeling-101-moroccan-tadelakt-walls)).

**Urushi lacquer**
- Urushi cures (polymerises) by taking up humidity: ideally 20 to 25°C and 70 to 80% RH, about 3 to 4 days per coat (kintsugi supplier guidance). — [Kintsugi-kit raw urushi](https://kintsugi-kit.com/products/raw-urushi-lacquer-for-kintsugi-and-lacquerware-projects)
- Ageing: microcracks form on aged urushi surfaces, helped by pinholes that form as relative humidity fluctuates (Imperial College PhD, Judith Thei). For conservation, keep RH stable at 50 to 60% and avoid swings. — [Imperial Spiral](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/handle/10044/1/9462); [JAIC 37(1) 1998](https://cool.culturalheritage.org/jaic/articles/jaic37-01-009_2.html)
- The claim that urushi "becomes more translucent over the years", revealing underlayers, is anecdotal (hobbyist forum) and unverified. — [Penturners forum](https://www.penturners.org/threads/manu-propria-namban-kawari-nuri.127333)

**Hagi ware ("seven transformations", Hagi no nanabake 萩の七化け)**
- Glaze and clay contract at different rates as the kiln cools, which produces crazing. The porous clay and the crazing absorb tea, so the colour shifts with use ("nanabake"), "as many as seven" changes. Changed pieces are prized. Hagi is one of the three classic tea-ceremony wares (with Raku and Karatsu) and descends from Korean potters brought to Hagi after the late-16th-century invasions. — [Japan MLIT Tagengo DB, Kikuya House Hagi ware text](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/H30-01082.html); [MLIT PDF](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001560037.pdf)
- "Seven" is a traditional saying, not a fixed count. — [MLIT](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/H30-01082.html)

### Inferences
- A "material library" card for each material could use four consistent fields: *Mechanism / First visible change / Settled state / Reset or repair*. For example, brass: oxidation plus skin oils / weeks to months / 1 to 5 years / polish to reset or leave alone. Copper: sulfate patina / brown in 1 to 3 years / green in about 20 to 30 years / generally left alone. Kaikado: 2 to 3 months (copper) to 3 to 5 years (tin). Hagi: changes with use, "up to seven" stages.
- Materials split into those where patina is *additive*, which clients should be told to leave alone (brass, copper, veg-tan, Hagi, yakisugi left to silver), and those where care is *renewal*, a ritual (soap oak, tadelakt re-soaping, re-oiled yakisugi, re-honed stone). Both fit "precision holds patina", but they suit different personas: hands-off versus ritual maintainer.
- Calcite surfaces (marble, travertine, lime, tadelakt) share one vulnerability, acid. A single "acid-free care" rule covers the whole family.

### Gaps
- No sources were retrieved this session for: walnut (it lightens and goes golden in UV, whereas oak darkens; background knowledge only), oiled oak specifically, bronze (cast-bronze patination and conservation waxing), hand-forged iron (oil/wax and rust bloom), linen, wool/felt/boiled wool, raw denim (fade timelines), rammed earth, terrazzo, washi, raku. These need targeted follow-up. Good primary sources would be Getty Conservation Institute pages, the Smithsonian MCI, Historic England technical guidance (lime, earth), and Japanese crafts sites (e.g. Japan Traditional Craft Industry Association / "Kogei Japan").
- No conservation-grade (peer-reviewed) timeline was found for indoor brass patina. All figures are from retailers.
- The urushi translucency claim is unverified.

## 3. Joinery and details: which joints and details express precision?

### Takeaway
The verified examples show the same idea at several scales. A joint that is precise enough to hold without glue, nails or concealment becomes the readable "ornament". Examples: Ise's nail-free cypress joinery, which is rebuilt every 20 years; Nakashima's butterfly keys stabilising cracks in slabs; Kaikado's self-closing lid; Hermès's two-needle saddle stitch; the resoleable Goodyear welt; Scarpa's Brion detailing.

### Cited Findings
- **Ise Jingū:** solid hinoki (cypress) buildings joined without nails, using dowels and interlocking joints. Rebuilt identically every 20 years (*shikinen sengū*, said to date from about 690 CE). The 62nd rebuild was in 2013. Old timber is reused, for example for the entrance torii or for other shrines. — [Wikipedia: Ise Shrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ise_Shrine); [Lonely Planet](https://www.nonprod.lonelyplanet.com/points-of-interest/ise-jingu/1509328)
- **Kigumi:** joinery that assembles wooden parts without nails, screws or metal fasteners (low-authority source). — [Arch2O](https://arch2o.com/?p=566948); [Open Culture on Japanese joinery](https://www.openculture.com/2020/12/the-art-of-traditional-japanese-wood-joinery.html)
- **Nakashima butterflies (bowtie keys):** a bowtie-shaped inlay of contrasting wood (often rosewood, sometimes walnut sapwood against dark walnut) that mends and stabilises cracks and splits, joins boards and stops cracks spreading, while also reading as ornament. — [Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/auction/2023/design/trestle-dining-table); [Moderne Gallery](https://modernegallery.com/works/furniture/tables/dining-tables/george-nakashima-conoid-dining-table-1984/); [Wikipedia: Butterfly joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly_joint)
- "Conoid" is the name of a Nakashima furniture line (from 1961), named after his Conoid Studio in New Hope, PA. It is not a joint type. — [Moderne Gallery](https://modernegallery.com/works/furniture/george-nakashima-conoid-dining-table-1965-mg1965/); [NGV conservator insight, Conoid bench](https://www.ngv.vic.gov.au/essay/conservator-insight-conoid-bench-1966/)
- **Scarpa, Brion Tomb (San Vito d'Altivole):** the main symbol is two interlocking circles (the entrance passes through a gap made by intertwined circles). The stepped, "pseudo-classical" relief moulding is called the most notable element of his "form grammar" there. The zigzag decoration repeats around the arcosolium. — [O2 Landscapes](https://www.o2landscapes.com/essays/brion-monumental-tomb/); [Architect Magazine](https://www.architectmagazine.com/Design/tattoos-and-tombs-carlo-scarpas-great-small-architecture_o/); [Maharam](https://www.maharam.com/stories/nielson_carlo-scarpa-brion-cemetery)
- **Castelvecchio, Verona (1956–64):** Scarpa's intervention in a medieval fabric (date range only). Detail specifics were not retrieved. — [UK Essays (low authority)](https://www.ukessays.com/essays/architecture/design-of-the-tomba-brion-vega-architecture-essay.php)
- **Hermès saddle stitch:** done by hand with two needles and waxed linen thread. Each stitch locks, so if one breaks the seam does not unravel (the locking point is standard saddlery knowledge; not explicitly in the snippet). — [Permanent Style](https://www.permanentstyle.com/2011/09/hermes-trying-the-saddle-stitch.html); [Why Is This Interesting](https://whyisthisinteresting.substack.com/p/the-saddle-stitch-edition)
- **Goodyear welt:** the sole is stitched to a welt rather than the upper, so it can be resoled many times. Counts vary widely and are not standardised: 2 to 4 (a brand), 5 to 10 (a retailer), "3" vs "20" (cobblers on StyleForum). The limit is usually the condition of the upper. — [Myrqvist](https://myrqvist.com/blogs/journal/what-is-a-goodyear-welt); [Paul Malone](https://paulmalone.com/blogs/news/embracing-excellence-the-legacy-of-goodyear-welted-shoes-by-paul-malone); [StyleForum](https://www.styleforum.net/threads/the-bespoke-shoes-thread.445708/page-253)
- **Kaikado:** see section 2. The lid's self-closing fit is a tolerance you can show a client. — [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaikado)

### Inferences
- Ise is the strongest single exemplar of "precision holds patina" in a time-based sense. Precision is held by *knowledge*, renewed every 20 years. The materials themselves are allowed to age and then pass on into reuse. (By the 20-year cycle, the next rebuild falls in 2033; this is inferred, not verified.)
- The butterfly key is the clearest furniture-scale image of the stance: a precise geometric inlay that *holds* a natural defect (a crack), and both are left visible.
- The Goodyear welt and the saddle stitch are "repair-ready precision". They are details designed so that the object can be renewed, which links directly to section 4.

### Gaps
- Not researched this session (no sources retrieved): sashimono specifics (e.g. Kyoto/Edo sashimono), named Japanese joints (e.g. kanawa tsugi, okkake daisen tsugi), dovetail and mortise-and-tenon history and tolerances, hand-rolled hems (e.g. Hermès carré scarves), Scarpa's Querini Stampalia details (water channel, entrance bridge) and Castelvecchio details (Cangrande setting, the gap between new and old). No numerical tolerance (mm) for Kaikado lids was found.

## 4. Repair culture: how does repair create value?

### Takeaway
Repair has moved from being a sign of necessity (boro) to a sign of value (kintsugi, Hermès, Patagonia Worn Wear), and it now happens at industrial scale. Patagonia carries out tens of thousands of repairs a year, and Hermès ateliers receive more than 100,000 items a year. The kintsugi origin story is legend, not documented history.

### Cited Findings
- **Kintsugi:** the origin is uncertain. The popular story involves shogun Ashikaga Yoshimasa (1436–1490) and a Chinese celadon bowl returned with metal staples. The bowl usually identified is "Bakōhan", a 13th-century Longquan celadon in the Tokyo National Museum. Lacquer repair goes back to the Jōmon period. Gold repair entering the tea room is sometimes credited to Furuta Oribe (late 16th to early 17th century). By the 16th century it was closely tied to tea culture. — [Tezumi](https://www.tezumi.com/blogs/tezumi-insights/kintsugi-history-and-introduction); [Musubi Kiln](https://musubikiln.com/blogs/journal/what-is-kintsugi-a-guide-to-japans-art-of-gold-repair); [AGGV](https://emagazine.aggv.ca/kintsugi-the-art-of-mending/)
- **Boro:** patched and pieced textiles from northern Japan (Aomori). It was too cold for cotton there, so hemp was the base cloth. Boro is tied to *mottainai* ("waste not"). After industrialisation some families destroyed their boro. It was revived through 1970s–80s folk-craft collecting. Folklorist Chūzaburō Tanaka's collection includes nearly 800 items designated Important Cultural Properties. The Amuse Museum (Asakusa, Tokyo) that showed it closed on 31 March 2019. — [Wikipedia: Amuse Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amuse_Museum); [Carryology](https://www.carryology.com/insights/carry-culture/how-boro-became-art-the-quiet-survival-of-japans-patched-cloth/); [Papersky](https://papersky.jp/en/boro/)
- **Patagonia Worn Wear:** the Reno facility, "the largest repair facility in North America", completes about 40,000 repairs a year (company figure, about 2017; about 30,000 around 2015). It had 42 to 45 full-time repair technicians. One report gives 92,042 garment and bag repairs in North America in FY2025, about 50% of mailed-in items handled in Reno. — [Patagonia blog 2018](https://www.patagonia.com/blog/2018/02/fixation/?print=1); [Snowboarder's Journal](https://thesnowboardersjournal.com/blogs/exclusives/patagonia-repairs); [Berkeley Haas](https://haas.berkeley.edu/responsible-business/blog/posts/patagonias-worn-wear-2017/)
- **Hermès repair:** the company says it has the skill "to maintain, repair and recreate" (Laurent Dordet). Ateliers receive "more than a hundred thousand items" a year, including reworking saddle stitches. — [CdT.ch "The perfection of Hermès"](https://www.cdt.ch/prodotti/hub/the-perfection-of-hermes-286054)

### Inferences
- Value is created through repair in three ways, which can be presented to clients: (1) *narrative* (kintsugi and boro make the repair visible), (2) *continuity* (Hermès and Patagonia make the object last longer and strengthen the brand relationship), (3) *designed repairability* (welt, saddle stitch, butterfly key, Ise's planned renewal).
- "Objects that improve with age" is a defensible claim only for materials with *additive* patina (section 2). For others, the honest claim is "can be renewed".

### Gaps

- Right-to-repair legislation was not researched. Examples include the EU Right to Repair Directive (adopted 2024) and US state laws. Check primary EU or legislative sources before citing.
- No primary source (an Hermès annual report or press release) was found confirming the 100,000+ figure or repair policy details. The figure is from a trade article.
- The FY2025 Patagonia figure was reported in search results without a confirmed primary URL. Verify it against Patagonia's impact reporting before citing.
